Among unsatisfied professionals, there’s a repeating theme — the belief that someday (a mysterious day in the future) things will ① unexpectedly change and they’ll stumble into the perfect job or career. At the same time, they (typically) take ②minimal action to meaningfully alter their current situation. They don’t like their job, so what do they do? They head to job boards and search for roles with an ③identical job title, hoping that the universe will sprinkle a bit of magic on their career if they just hit the refresh button in a different department or company. Efforts like this make professionals feel like they are doing something, when really they’re just setting themselves up to take a new role and quickly become just as ④inspired as they were before. It’s like rearranging deck chairs on the Titanic. You’ve got to ⑤revolutionize your approach. If you keep playing the same game, you’ll win the same prizes.

Sociologists argue that our perception and usage of time are not just basic properties of nature, like a clock ticking away in the background, nor are they simply psychological phenomena of individual experience. Instead, how we perceive and understand time is to a large degree shaped by tradition and cultural norms. Eviatar Zerubavel’s work, The Seven Day Circle: The History and Meaning of the Week, demonstrates how the week, a fundamental unit of time, is a social invention that varies across cultures, influencing how people structure their activities and rhythms of life. Why do we consider a nine-to-five job routine? Why don’t many of us work on the weekends? Why do we usually pay our bills at the end of the month? Why is the retirement age sixty-five? These numbers and patterns aren’t really dictated by nature or any essential human characteristic, but are rather social creations that we internalize and come to accept as “normal.”

These numbers and patterns aren’t really dictated by nature or any essential human characteristic, but are rather social creations that we internalize and come to accept as “normal.” (이런 숫자와 패턴은 자연이나 인간의 본질적 특성이 정한 게 아니라, 우리가 내면화해서 결국 ‘정상’으로 받아들이게 된 사회적 창조물이에요.)

Sociologists argue that our perception and usage of time are not just basic properties of nature, like a clock ㉠[tick] away in the background, nor are they simply psychological phenomena of individual experience. ④ ㉣ : retire → retirement → 은퇴 (명사)

㉢은 앞 문장 전체 내용이 이어서 "영향을 준다"는 뜻이에요.

문제 본문 다시 보기
In 1977, psychologists Lynn Hasher and David Goldstein asked a group of college students how certain they were that sixty general-knowledge trivia-like statements were either true or false. The students were asked to evaluate the sixty statements three times, at two-week intervals. Crucially, only twenty of the statements were repeated each time; the other forty were new. The researchers then compared the ‘truth-ratings’ of the repeated versus non-repeated statements. What they found was astonishing. __________, belief in the truth of the claim went up as a function of repetition. In other words, the more often you hear a statement, the more ‘true’ it sounds. This became known as the illusory truth effect.
정답: ②

보기와 우리말 뜻

① Only when statements were mathematically and logically proven → 진술이 수학적·논리적으로 입증되었을 때만

② Regardless of whether the statement was true or false → 진술이 참이든 거짓이든 상관없이

③ Because students deliberately researched each statement's factual basis → 학생들이 각 진술의 사실 근거를 일부러 조사했기 때문에

④ If participants possessed prior professional expertise in trivia topics → 참가자들이 상식 주제에 전문 지식이 있었다면

⑤ Although the researchers strictly warned students against deception → 연구자들이 학생들에게 속임에 대해 엄격히 경고했지만

답의 근거 (본문)

In other words, the more often you hear a statement, the more ‘true’ it sounds. (다시 말해, 어떤 진술을 더 자주 들을수록 그것은 더 '사실'처럼 들려요.)

What they found was astonishing. (그들이 발견한 것은 놀라웠어요.)
